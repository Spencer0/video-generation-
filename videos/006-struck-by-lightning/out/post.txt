Sources (for replies):
- Roy Sullivan: 7 recorded strikes 1942-1977, Guinness World Records. All are his accounts, documented by the Shenandoah superintendent (who never witnessed one).
  1942 lookout tower: burn down his leg, hole in his shoe. 1969: through his truck window, eyebrows burned off. Hair caught fire on several later strikes (1972, 1973, 1976 per Guinness).
  He kept a can of water handy for his hair (Wikipedia, citing Friedman, "Out of the Blue", 2008). 1977: struck while fishing; he said he then fought off a bear trying to take his trout, with a branch (his story).
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Sullivan
  https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2023/1/incredible-story-of-man-who-survived-being-struck-by-lightning-seven-times-733932
- Lee Trevino: struck at the 1975 Western Open, with Jerry Heard and Bobby Nichols; the "1-iron" line is his joke (do not actually do it).
  https://barrypopik.com/blog/not_even_god_can_hit
- Flashover: a return-stroke pulse lasts tens of microseconds; most current flows over the wet skin, which is why most people survive. Some does go through, which is what stops hearts.
- Cars: a hard-topped metal vehicle with windows up is safe because the shell carries current around you (NWS).
- Lichtenberg figures: fern-shaped skin marks, not burns or scars; they usually fade within hours to about a day.
  https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s12024-023-00612-7
- What kills: cardiac/respiratory arrest; CPR saves lives, and victims carry no charge, so it is safe to touch them.
- Odds: US National Weather Service, 1 in 1,222,000 per year, 1 in 15,300 over 80 years (2009-2018 data); about 10% of people struck die.
  https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-odds
- 7 strikes "by pure luck": with lifetime rate L = 1/15,300, P(7 or more in one life) ~ L^7/7! ~ 1 in 10^33 (Poisson, average American). Naive on purpose: Roy's exposure was nowhere near average.
- "When thunder roars, go indoors" is the NWS slogan.
